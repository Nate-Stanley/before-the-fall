Why This Matters

Unintentional falls drive approximately 8.9 million emergency department visits annually in the United States. Validated prevention approaches exist, but consistent delivery does not — leaving EDs to absorb the downstream burden. Across five community events spanning rural and metro Oregon from 2023 to 2026, our team identified a large, underserved population of older adults with substantial fall risk and limited prior prevention engagement. Despite nearly universal concern about falling, most had never discussed it with a primary care provider and had never attended a prevention class.

After a single community event, fall-prevention class attendance tripled by 6-month follow-up. High-risk participants experienced post-event falls at seven times the rate of those below the risk threshold, underscoring both the clinical significance of accurate identification and the potential impact of earlier intervention. These findings provide feasibility, targeting, and outcome-rate estimates to support a future hybrid Type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial assessing whether upstream community-based fall prevention can serve as a bridge to reduce fall-related ED visits and downstream acute care burden.

Before the Fall: Connecting High-Risk Adults to Prevention Before Emergency Department Visits
Nathaniel Stanley, BS; Sarah Gold, RN, BSN, CEN; Bory Kea, MD, MCR, FACEP; Heather Wong, MHS, BSN, RN, TCRN; Apoorva Salvi, BS; Ren Rosz, MD, MPH; Jody Berryhill, RN, BSN, MN; Nicole B. Riley, BS; Rebecca Brown, RN, BSN, MN, CCRN; Hiroko Kiyoshi-Teo, RN, PhD
Study Objectives Unintentional falls drive approximately 8.9 million emergency department (ED) visits annually in the United States. Validated prevention exists; consistent delivery does not — saddling EDs with the downstream burden. To address this, our emergency department, trauma program, and school of nursing designed and piloted an upstream community-based fall prevention program to reach high-risk, community-dwelling adults outside routine care through walk-in events. We aimed to characterize participants’ fall risk and prior fall-prevention engagement, assess longitudinal changes in prevention behavior and fall outcomes, and evaluate the feasibility of recruitment, retention, and follow-up to support a future hybrid Type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial.
Methods We conducted a prospective observational pilot study at five walk-in community fall prevention events held from 2023 to 2026, spanning both rural and metro areas, staffed by trained volunteers and nursing students with support from emergency department and nursing faculty. All adults aged 18 and older were eligible and invited to enroll. Enrolled participants consented to retain event-day assessment data, access to electronic health records, and 6-month follow-up surveys. Regardless of consent status, all attendees received validated screenings — including the CDC’s Stopping Elderly Accidents, Deaths and Injuries (STEADI) fall-risk screening and the Short Falls Efficacy Scale-International (Short FES-I) — followed by brief motivational interviewing, individualized prevention education, and local resource referrals. STEADI scores ≥4 defined high fall risk; Short FES-I scores were categorized as low (7–8), moderate (9–13), or high concern (14–28).
Results Of 193 attendees across five events, 64 adults (mean age 73.6 ± 9.8 years; 75.0% female) enrolled. At enrollment, 42/63 (66.7%) screened as high fall risk, and 35/64 (54.7%) reported a fall in the prior year, including 32/42 (76.2%) of high-risk participants. 37/64 (57.8%) reported no prior PCP fall-prevention discussion. Additionally, 56/64 (87.5%) had never attended a fall-prevention class, and 55/64 (85.9%) reported moderate-to-high concern about falling. Of 52 eligible participants, 28/52 (53.8%) completed 6-month follow-up. Among paired respondents, fall-prevention class attendance increased from 4/26 (15.4%) at baseline to 12/26 (46.2%) by 6 months. Chart-documented post-event falls occurred in 15/52 (28.8%). Falls were more common among high-risk participants than those below threshold (13/33, 39.4%, vs. 1/18, 5.6%; RR 7.09, 95% CI 1.01–49.9). Fall-, balance-, or dizziness-related ED or urgent care visits occurred in 5/52 (9.6%).
Conclusion Two-thirds of community-dwelling participants screened high-risk. Despite nearly all expressing concern about falling, most reported not having a fall-prevention discussion with their PCP. Post-event falls occurred at seven times the rate among high-risk participants compared to low-risk participants. Fall-prevention class attendance more than doubled after the event. These findings suggest our community-based fall-prevention model identified and reached adults with substantial fall risk, high concern about falling, and limited prior prevention engagement, while delivering meaningful evidence-based prevention. This pilot provided feasibility, targeting, and outcome-rate estimates to power a future hybrid Type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial assessing whether upstream community-based fall prevention can serve as a bridge to reduce fall-related ED visits and downstream acute care burden.

Methods
Instruments and procedures
  • STEADI 12-item questionnaire — Score ≥4 = high fall risk. CDC’s evidence-based screen–assess–intervene framework. Administered to all attendees regardless of consent status.
  • Short FES-I (Short Falls Efficacy Scale–International) — 7-item concern-about-falling scale. Scores categorized as low (7–8), moderate (9–13), or high concern (14–28).
  • Prior engagement items — Self-reported PCP fall-prevention discussion, fall-prevention class attendance, and falls in the prior year.
  • Performance battery — Timed Up and Go, 30-second sit-to-stand, 4-stage balance test, orthostatic blood pressure.
  • Electronic health record review — Chart review confirmed pre- and post-event fall-prevention engagement, risk factors, and fall outcomes.
  • 6-month follow-up survey — 28/52 eligible participants (53.8%) completed follow-up. Assessed fall-prevention class attendance, falls, and PCP engagement.

All attendees received brief motivational interviewing, individualized prevention education, and warm hand-offs to local fall-prevention programs regardless of enrollment status. Events were staffed by trained volunteers and nursing students with support from ED and nursing faculty.
